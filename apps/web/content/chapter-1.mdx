## A dollar in, a dollar out

The federal government collects money and it spends money. The Bureau of the
Fiscal Service, part of the Treasury, counts both and publishes the count once
a month in the *Monthly Treasury Statement*.<Ref id="mts-report" />

Two words carry most of this chapter. A <Term id="concept.receipt">**receipt**</Term>
is money the government collects from the public by exercising its power to tax
or otherwise compel payment: income taxes, payroll taxes, excise taxes, customs
duties, court fines, and the Federal Reserve's deposits of
earnings.<Ref id="gao-glossary" /> An <Term id="concept.outlay">**outlay**</Term>
is money that actually leaves — a check issued, an electronic transfer sent,
cash paid — to settle something the government already owes.<Ref id="gao-glossary" />

Subtract the second from the first and you get the third word. When outlays
exceed receipts the difference is the <Term id="concept.deficit">**deficit**</Term>;
when receipts exceed outlays it is the surplus.<Ref id="gao-glossary" />

So far this fiscal year, receipts are
<Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.receipts.total" period="fiscal_ytd" />, outlays are
<Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.outlays.total" period="fiscal_ytd" />, and the gap
between them is
<Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.deficit.total" period="fiscal_ytd" sign="absolute" />.

</Step>

<Step id="the-fiscal-year" stage="whole">

## The year is not the year

The government's books do not run January to December. The federal
<Term id="concept.fiscal_year">**fiscal year**</Term> begins on October 1 and
ends on September 30, and it is named for the calendar year in which it ends —
fiscal 2026 began on October 1, 2025.<Ref id="gao-glossary" /> Everything on
this page labeled "fiscal year to date" is counted from October 1 forward.

Most other numbers a reader meets are on calendar years. A fiscal-year total
and a calendar-year total of the same thing are two different measurements, so
Penny labels which one it is showing and never adds them together.

</Step>

## Where it comes from

The Monthly Treasury Statement sorts receipts by source. These are the sources
it names, with live figures for the fiscal year to date.<Ref id="mts-report" />

- Individual income taxes: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.receipts.category.individual_income_tax" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Social insurance and retirement receipts: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.receipts.category.social_insurance_retirement" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Corporation income taxes: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.receipts.category.corporation_income_tax" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Customs duties: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.receipts.category.customs_duties" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Excise taxes: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.receipts.category.excise_taxes" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Estate and gift taxes: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.receipts.category.estate_and_gift_taxes" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Miscellaneous receipts: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.receipts.category.miscellaneous_receipts" period="fiscal_ytd" />

Those are net figures. Treasury deducts refunds from gross
collections,<Ref id="mts-report" /> which has a consequence you will meet in a
chart: a source can print below zero for a month in which refunds exceeded what
was collected. The diagram draws that backwards rather than hiding it.

"Miscellaneous receipts" is a real category, not a rounding bucket. The Federal
Reserve's deposits of earnings are reported on a line of their own inside
it.<Ref id="mts-report" />

</Step>

<Step id="the-payroll-taxes" stage="receipts-social-insurance">

## The payroll taxes

Social insurance and retirement receipts are the taxes withheld from wages and
paid by employers, plus unemployment insurance taxes and federal retirement
contributions. Treasury reports the three parts separately. They are credited to
<Term id="concept.trust_fund">**trust fund accounts**</Term> — chiefly Social
Security's and Medicare's.<Ref id="mts-report" />

A federal trust fund is an account that a law designates as one. The
Government Accountability Office's budget glossary is explicit about what that
does and does not mean: except in rare circumstances, "a trust fund account
imposes no fiduciary responsibility on the federal
government."<Ref id="gao-glossary" />

Those receipts are counted in the same governmental-receipts total as every
other tax,<Ref id="mts-report" /> so on the diagram they are a strand of the
inbound flow, not a separate pot beside it.

</Step>

<Step id="the-account-it-lands-in" stage="tga">

## The account it lands in

Cash moves before the books close. Nearly every federal dollar passes through
the <Term id="fiscal.tga.closing_balance">**Treasury General Account**</Term>,
the government's operating account at the Federal Reserve, which acts as the
Treasury's fiscal agent. When the Treasury pays out of that account, funds move
to a bank; when a tax payment arrives, funds move the other
way.<Ref id="frb-liabilities" /> Treasury publishes the balance every business
day in the *Daily Treasury Statement*.<Ref id="dts-operating-cash" />

The latest published closing balance is
<Num seriesId="fiscal.tga.closing_balance" period="day" />.

<Aside id="cash-versus-books" title="The cash balance and the budget figures are different measurements">

The Daily Treasury Statement counts cash in and out of an account. The Monthly
Treasury Statement counts budget receipts and outlays on a modified cash basis:
receipts on the basis of collections, outlays on the basis of checks issued,
transfers sent, or cash paid, and interest on the publicly held debt on an
accrual basis.<Ref id="mts-report" />

The two will not tie to each other, and Penny never nets one against the other.
They appear side by side, each labeled with what it measures.

</Aside>

</Step>

<Step id="where-it-goes" stage="outlays">

## Where it goes, and three words that are not synonyms

An outlay is money out the door. Two words that sound like it are not it, and
keeping them apart is the difference between a right answer and a
plausible-looking wrong one.

<Term id="concept.budget_authority">**Budget authority**</Term> is permission,
granted by law, to enter into financial obligations that will result in outlays
now or later.<Ref id="gao-glossary" />

An <Term id="concept.obligation">**obligation**</Term> is a commitment — a
binding legal liability created when an agency places an order, signs a
contract, or awards a grant. Payment may follow immediately or years
later.<Ref id="gao-glossary" />

An outlay is the payment itself. Outlays in one fiscal year may settle
obligations incurred in earlier ones.<Ref id="gao-glossary" />

This chapter counts outlays, because the Monthly Treasury Statement counts
outlays. USASpending.gov counts
obligations.<Ref id="usaspending-glossary" /> The two answer different
questions, and their totals will not match. Penny stores them as different
accounting concepts so nothing can quietly add them together.

</Step>

<Step id="sorted-by-purpose" stage="outlays">

## Sorted by what it is for

Outlays can be sorted by which agency spent them or by what they were spent on.
The Monthly Treasury Statement publishes both. The flow diagram uses the second
— the <Term id="concept.functional_classification">**functional
classification**</Term>, which the Government Accountability Office describes as
grouping budget authority, outlays, receipts, and tax expenditures "according to
the national needs being addressed."<Ref id="gao-glossary" />

Each budget account sits in the single function that best reflects its major
purpose, regardless of which agency runs it, so that the functions add up to the
budget totals.<Ref id="gao-glossary" /> That last clause is why the diagram can
be drawn at all.

<SankeyStage focus="outlays-functions" />

- National defense: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.outlays.category.national_defense" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Social Security: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.outlays.category.social_security" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Medicare: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.outlays.category.medicare" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Health: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.outlays.category.health" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Income security: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.outlays.category.income_security" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Net interest: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.outlays.category.net_interest" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Veterans benefits and services: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.outlays.category.veterans_benefits_and_services" period="fiscal_ytd" />

A function is not an agency, and the boundaries are drawn by rule, not by
intuition. Medicare is its own function and is excluded from Health; health care
for military personnel sits in National Defense, and health care for veterans in
Veterans Benefits and Services.<Ref id="gao-glossary" />

</Step>

<Step id="negative-spending" stage="outlays-functions">

## Some spending runs backwards

Outlays are stated net of offsetting collections and of
refunds.<Ref id="mts-report" /> A function that collects more of those in a
period than it spends prints a net outlay below zero.

<SankeyStage focus="outlays-negative" />

One function is normally negative by construction.
<Term id="fiscal.mts.outlays.category.undistributed_offsetting_receipts">**Undistributed
offsetting receipts**</Term> holds collections that Treasury deducts from the
government-wide total rather than from any single agency or function: agencies'
payments into their employees' retirement funds, interest received by trust
funds, rents and royalties on the Outer Continental Shelf, and certain other
interest.<Ref id="mts-report" /> The Government Accountability Office gives the
reason — deducting them anywhere else would distort the agency and subfunction
totals.<Ref id="gao-glossary" />

Its current figure is
<Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.outlays.category.undistributed_offsetting_receipts" period="fiscal_ytd" />.

The diagram draws this as a flow running the other way. Dropping it would be
tidier and wrong: the remaining categories would stop adding up to the published
total.

</Step>

<Step id="two-interest-numbers" stage="outlays-net-interest">

## Two different interest numbers

Net interest is budget function 900: transactions that give rise to interest
payments or income, including interest paid on the public debt, offset by
interest collections.<Ref id="gao-glossary" /> It is what the budget records as
an outlay.

Treasury separately publishes
<Term id="fiscal.debt.interest_expense_total">**interest expense on the public
debt**</Term> — the cost of borrowing, reported monthly and cumulatively for the
fiscal year.<Ref id="interest-expense" />

- Net interest, an outlay: <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.outlays.category.net_interest" period="fiscal_ytd" />
- Interest expense on the public debt: <Num seriesId="fiscal.debt.interest_expense_total" period="fiscal_ytd" />

They are computed differently and they do not match. Penny holds them as two
series flagged as not comparable, and the site will not chart them on one axis
or add them together. The reason is printed wherever both appear.

</Step>

<Step id="the-gap" stage="deficit">

## The gap is not a category

Receipts minus outlays. That is the whole calculation.

The deficit is not something the government buys. It is the arithmetic
difference between two other flows, which is why the diagram draws it as a
balancing flow rather than as one more destination for spending. Sorting the
outlay side does not move it; only the two totals move it.

For the latest month, receipts were
<Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.receipts.total" period="month" />, outlays were
<Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.outlays.total" period="month" />, and the difference
was <Num seriesId="fiscal.mts.deficit.total" period="month" />.

</Step>

<Step id="deficit-is-not-debt" stage="deficit-vs-debt">

## The deficit is a flow; the debt is a level

A deficit is measured over a period — a month, a fiscal year. The
<Term id="fiscal.debt.total_public_debt_outstanding">**debt**</Term> is a level
at a moment. Treasury publishes total public debt outstanding every business
day, in Debt to the Penny; it is the sum of debt held by the public and
intragovernmental holdings.<Ref id="debt-to-the-penny" />

<Num seriesId="fiscal.debt.total_public_debt_outstanding" period="day" />, as of
the latest business day.

The two do not move one-for-one. The Government Accountability Office calls the
ways a deficit gets covered the
<Term id="concept.means_of_financing">**means of financing**</Term>: Treasury
borrowing, drawing down Treasury cash balances, the sale of gold, seigniorage,
cash flows through credit financing accounts, letting certain unpaid liabilities
rise, and other similar transactions. It is customary, GAO writes, to split the
total into the change in debt held by the public — the primary means of
financing — and everything else.<Ref id="gao-glossary" /> The Monthly Treasury
Statement reports these in a table of its own.<Ref id="mts-report" />

So the change in the debt over a month and the deficit for that month are
related numbers, not the same number, and Penny will not print one as the other.

</Step>

<Step id="why-a-month-moves" stage="calendar">

## Why a month moves

Some month-to-month movement is the calendar rather than a change in policy.

Benefit payments due on the first of a month are made on the last business day
before it when the first is not a business day, which shifts outlays out of one
month and into the one before. Treasury flags these shifts in the statement
itself: the July 2026 report notes that military active-duty and retirement pay,
veterans benefits, Supplemental Security Income, and some Medicare payments
moved into July because August 1 fell on a non-business day.<Ref id="mts-report" />

Receipts move for the mirror-image reason. They cluster around tax due dates,
and Treasury cites the absence of a major corporate or individual due date when
it explains a light month.<Ref id="mts-report" />

Comparing a month to the same month a year earlier removes some of this;
comparing fiscal year to date against the same span of the prior year removes
more. Neither removes all of it, and Penny says which comparison it is making.

</Step>

<Step id="what-numbers-do-after-publication" stage="whole">

## What the numbers do after they are published

The Monthly Treasury Statement is normally released on the eighth workday of
the month following the month it reports, at a time printed in each
issue.<Ref id="mts-report" /> Debt to the Penny is updated at the end of each
business day with the previous business day's figure.<Ref id="debt-to-the-penny" />

Published values change. Sources revise. Three rules govern how this page
handles that:

- A revised value is stored as a new row and never overwrites the old one, so
  the site can always show what was known at a given time.
- A period with nothing published renders as a gap. It is never a zero and never
  the previous period's number held over.
- Every figure carries its source, its dataset, the period it describes, the
  date it was published, and its unit as published.

</Step>

<Step id="what-this-chapter-does-not-say" stage="whole">

## What this chapter does not tell you

Three things are deliberately absent.

**How the borrowing is done.** The auctions, who buys, and at what yield are
the machinery behind the "Treasury borrowing" line in the means of
financing.<Ref id="gao-glossary" /> They are the subject of a later chapter.

**What was promised versus what was paid.** This page counts outlays.
Obligations — the commitments behind them — are reported on
USASpending.gov<Ref id="usaspending-glossary" /> and join Penny only behind a
stated bridge between the two concepts.

**What happens next.** The Congressional Budget Office publishes baseline
projections of federal spending, revenues, deficits and debt, built on the
assumption that current law generally stays as it is, in its recurring *Budget
and Economic Outlook*.<Ref id="cbo-baseline" /> Where those appear on Penny they
are labeled as CBO's projections, stored under a separate accounting concept,
and never summed or charted with observed Treasury figures.

This chapter describes mechanics. Whether the resulting path is a problem is a
question people disagree about in public and at length; that disagreement is
worth reporting with names attached, and it is not on this page.

</Step>
